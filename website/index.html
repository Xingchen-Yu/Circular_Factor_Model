U.S. House of Representatives · 1987 to 2026

The House no longer votes along a straight line.

We place every House member on a circle using every roll-call vote since 1987. Members who vote alike sit close together. On some votes the members at both ends of the House join each other against the middle, and when that happens often, the circle closes. Since 2011, it has.

The trend

How polarized is the House?

The score measures how far apart members sit around the circle. When the parties pull apart, the members at the far ends curve around until they begin to meet each other at the back. So the same number tells two stories: how polarized the House is, and how far it has bent from a line into a circle.

The score held steady through the 1990s and 2000s, dipped in the 111th Congress, and jumped to record highs from the 112th (2011) on. It is highest in the 117th and 118th.

What is this score?

Put every member on a circle, close to the members they vote with. The score is how spread out they are: 0 means everyone sits at one point, 1 means members are spread evenly all the way around. Statisticians call it the circular variance. Unlike most measures of polarization, it can be compared from one Congress to the next.

Inside each party

Democrats moved closer together. Republicans spread out.

The same score, measured within each party. In the late 1980s Democrats were the more varied party; as many conservative Southern Democrats retired or switched parties in the 1990s, Democrats came to vote more and more alike. Republicans moved the other way. Since 2011 they have been clearly more spread out than Democrats in every Congress, as some members break with their party's leadership on key votes.

Members who switched party count with the party they cast most of their votes with in that Congress. Members who left a party to become independent count with the party they left. See Methods.

When the ends meet

Some votes join the two ends against the middle

In March 2020 the House renewed federal surveillance powers in the USA FREEDOM Reauthorization Act. Leaders of both parties supported it and it passed 278 to 136. Most of the no votes came from the two ends of the House: members on the left worried about civil liberties, and members on the right worried about government overreach.

Votes like this are why a circle fits the modern House better than a line. On a line, the two ends are as far apart as possible. On a circle, they can be neighbors.

        Methods & data

        How the circle is made

        1. Start from every recorded vote. For each Congress we take every roll-call vote on the House floor, from the public Voteview database.
        2. Place members and votes on a circle. Each member gets a position on the circle. Each vote gets a "yes" point and a "no" point. Members tend to vote for whichever point is closer to them.
        3. Let the data find the positions. A statistical model finds the positions that best explain all the votes at once, and how uncertain each one is.
        4. Read off ranks and the score. Ranks go around the circle from the liberal end to the conservative end. The polarization score measures how spread out members are.

        Why a circle and not a line?

        The usual approach puts members on a line from left to right. A line cannot show members at both ends voting together against the middle, which happens often in the modern House on surveillance, war powers and spending. A circle can, because its two ends can bend toward each other. When the data shows little of this behavior, the circle bends very little and works like a line. Model comparison in the paper finds the line fits about as well or better before 2011 and the circle fits clearly better from 2011 on.

        How to read the numbers

        Rank
        Order around the circle, from the liberal end (1) to the conservative end. Ranks count the members estimated directly, so a House with 442 members may rank them out of 428 (see "placed members" below).
        95% range
        The member's rank falls in this range with 95% probability under the model.
        "More liberal than X% of the House"
        The share of members ranked closer to the conservative end, from the member's most likely rank.
        Polarization score
        The circular variance of member positions, between 0 and 1. Within-party scores use only that party's members.

        Things to keep in mind

        • Positions are not comparable across Congresses. Each Congress is estimated on its own, and its circle can be turned or flipped without changing the fit. Compare ranks or the polarization score instead.
        • The 119th Congress is in progress. It includes votes through .
        • Members who missed many votes. Members who missed more than 40% of votes are placed after the main estimate from the votes they did cast, and ranked among the others. Their ranges are wider. Members with fewer than 25 votes are not estimated.
        • Party switchers appear once per Congress, under the party they cast most of their votes with. For the within-party scores, members who left a party to become independent count with the party they left (Justin Amash in the 116th, Kevin Kiley in the 119th), or with the party they joined if they joined the other party in the same Congress (Virgil Goode in the 107th). Bernie Sanders (independent, 1991 to 2007) is left out of both party scores.
        • Non-voting delegates (D.C., Puerto Rico and the territories) are not included.
        • Pat Schroeder, 103rd Congress. The model finds two possible positions for her: rank about 80 (shown, the better fit) and near the liberal end. Her range is shown from the better-fitting one.

        Technical details

        Bayesian circular factor model (Yu and Rodríguez, 2021). Member positions βi and the yes and no positions of each vote live on the circle. The probability of a yes vote depends on the difference between the squared geodesic distances to the no and yes positions. Sampled with geodesic Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in a JAX implementation on GPU, with two to four chains per Congress. Chains that agree are pooled. Where one chain fits clearly better (log-likelihood more than three within-chain standard deviations higher), it is used alone; each such choice was checked against members' voting records. Every step, script and diagnostic is documented in the code repository.
