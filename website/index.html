        Methods & data

        How it works

        1. Take every recorded House vote since 1987.
        2. Place members on a circle so that those who vote alike sit close together.
        3. Rank them around the circle, from the liberal end to the conservative end.

        Why a circle?

        A line can't show both ends voting together against the middle. A circle can. When that rarely happens, the circle barely bends and acts like a line.

        Keep in mind

        • Compare ranks across Congresses, not positions.
        • The 119th Congress is in session: votes through .
        • Members who missed many votes have wide ranges. Under 25 votes: not estimated.
        • Non-voting delegates (D.C., territories) aren't included.
        Details for statisticians

        Model. Bayesian circular factor model (Yu & Rodríguez, 2021): members and each vote's yes and no positions live on a circle; a yes is likelier when the yes position is geodesically closer. Fitted with geodesic HMC (JAX, GPU), 2–4 chains per Congress. Agreeing chains are pooled; where one chain's log-likelihood is more than 3 within-chain SD higher, it is used alone, checked against voting records.

        Scores. Polarization score = circular variance of member positions; within-party scores use one party's members.

        Ranks. Out of the members fitted directly. Members missing more than 40% of votes are placed afterwards and ranked among them.

        Party switchers. Listed under the party they cast most votes with that Congress. For within-party scores, members who left for independent count with the party they left (Amash, 116th), or the party they joined that Congress (Goode, 107th). Sanders is excluded.

        Pat Schroeder, 103rd. A second, slightly worse-fitting position near the liberal end exists; the better fit (rank ~80) is shown.
